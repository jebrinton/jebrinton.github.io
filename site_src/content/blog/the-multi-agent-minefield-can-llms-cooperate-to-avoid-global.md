<p id="block0"><i>ArXiv paper </i><span><span><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12316"><i>here</i></a></span></span><i>.</i></p><p id="block1">Most AI safety research asks a familiar question: Will a single model behave safely? But many of the risks we actually worry about – including arms races, coordination failures, and runaway competition – don’t involve one single AI model acting alone. They emerge when multiple advanced AI systems interact.</p><p id="block2">This post summarizes the findings of <strong>GT-HarmBench</strong>, a paper that shifts the lens of AI safety from isolated agents to multi-agent strategic interaction – <span><span><a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14143">multi-agent safety</a></span></span>. Instead of asking whether an LLM makes good decisions in a vacuum, we ask a more deliberate question: can LLMs coordinate with each other when cooperation is the only way to avoid disaster?</p><h2 id="TL_DR">TL;DR</h2><ul><li id="block3"><strong>The Problem:</strong> LLMs are increasingly being used to support decision-makers IRL. In high-stakes scenarios, an overreliance on LLMs could lead to catastrophic outcomes.</li><li id="block4"><strong>Our motivation:</strong> As a first step towards a solution, we tried benchmarking whether LLMs generally nudge decision-makers towards utility-maximizing outcomes. Our experiments gave us a deeper understanding of this threat model.</li><li id="block5"><strong>Setup:</strong> We mapped <strong>2,009 high-stakes AI risk scenarios</strong> from the <span><span><a href="https://airisk.mit.edu/">MIT AI Risk Repository</a></span></span> onto six classic 2×2 games like the Prisoner&#x27;s Dilemma and Chicken. Such 2×2 games are easy to analyze: we can compute Nash equilibria and utility-maximizing outcomes and evaluate whether a model, when playing against a copy of itself, successfully lands in those cells.</li><li id="block6"><strong>Key Findings:</strong><ul><li id="block7"><strong>Cooperation rates:</strong> Models reach the utility-maximizing outcome in 62% of the time, the random baseline being 25%. In prisoner’s dilemma scenarios, the utility-maximizing outcome – i.e., the (cooperate, cooperate) cell – was reached 44% of times. This is comparable to human baselines (humans cooperate in prisoner’s dilemmas <span><span><a href="https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-1-4614-7883-6_557-2?">40-60%</a></span></span> of times).</li><li id="block8"><strong>Anchoring effects:</strong> Telling a model the explicit numerical payoffs (i.e. having a game-theoretic framing) increases the likelihood of reaching a Nash equilibrium; it reduces the likelihood of the utilitarian outcome.</li><li id="block9"><strong>Nudging works:</strong> Prompt engineering (pre-pending the game narratives with a suitable prompt) increases the likelihood of optimal outcomes.</li></ul></li><li id="block10"><strong>Bottom line:</strong> Agents may be safe in isolation, but this doesn’t mean a world run by autonomous agents would be safe.</li></ul><p id="block11"><img src="../assets/blog/the-multi-agent-minefield-can-llms-cooperate-to-avoid-global/figure-1.webp" metrics, and modify the original settings to promote higher social welfare.</i></p><h2 id="Methodology__Mapping_Catastrophes_to_Payoff_Matrices">Methodology: Mapping Catastrophes to Payoff Matrices</h2><p id="block12">Suppose an AI is acting as a counselor to decision-makers. How do we measure whether it generally nudges decision-makers towards utility-maximizing outcomes? We broke it down into four steps, focusing on advising in realistic, high-stakes scenarios:</p><p id="block13"><strong id="Step_one">Step one</strong></p><p id="block14">We started with the <strong>MIT AI Risk Repository</strong>, extracting 2,009 scenarios involving everything from autonomous weapons arms races to election manipulation.</p><p id="block15"><strong id="Step_two">Step two</strong></p><p id="block16">We mapped these real-world scenarios onto <strong>six canonical 2×2 games</strong>: Prisoner’s Dilemma, Chicken, Stag Hunt, Battle of the Sexes, Coordination, and No Conflict. These games describe strategic tensions in a wide range of real-world scenarios:</p><ul><li id="block17"><strong>Prisoner’s Dilemma:</strong> If we both cooperate (disarm), we are both safe. But if I disarm and you don&#x27;t, I lose everything. We both end up defecting (staying armed), leaving us both worse off than if we had just trusted each other.</li><li id="block18"><strong>Chicken:</strong> Two cars racing toward a cliff. The first to swerve is a &quot;chicken&quot; (loses face), but if neither swerves, both die. It’s a game of seeing who blinks first in a crisis.</li><li id="block19"><strong>Stag Hunt:</strong> We can catch a &quot;Stag&quot; (a huge win) if we both work together. But if one of us gets distracted by a &quot;Hare&quot; (a small, guaranteed solo win), the Stag escapes and the other person gets nothing. It&#x27;s about the risk of trusting a partner.</li><li id="block20"><strong>Battle of the Sexes:</strong> I want to go to the Opera; you want to go to the Boxing match. We both prefer being together to being alone, but we have different ideas about where the &quot;best&quot; outcome lies.</li><li id="block21"><strong>Coordination:</strong> It doesn&#x27;t matter if we drive on the left or the right side of the road, as long as we both pick the same side. The only &quot;wrong&quot; answer is disagreement.</li><li id="block22"><strong>No Conflict:</strong> Our interests are perfectly aligned. What is best for me is also best for you. This serves as our &quot;control&quot; to see if the AI can handle even the simplest win-win scenarios.</li></ul><p id="block23">In these games, two players can choose between two actions, yielding a total of four outcomes. Such games, which can be represented by means of <i>payoff matrices</i>:</p><p id="block24"><img src="../assets/blog/the-multi-agent-minefield-can-llms-cooperate-to-avoid-global/figure-2.webp" srcSet="https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/mgrvpr3d9voxr5zuy9sf 160w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/jrgjms1udub5ijlujwym 320w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/rzyqhyht0uwh29i4ho9n 480w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/qvvtibvyfewe81tblhq1 640w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/pjwdcxu0neowybignhut 800w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/bhy7kibuxqysxubqrvaa 960w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/ioidvtruudj06yl6yh9x 1120w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/qdejlfg4y7buxekdj6nz 1280w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/hlvegtrl2pvvkrjwh5pc 1440w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/ltj85h4vymlqrh2n2eaz 1600w"/ width="1600" height="1059" alt="Multi-Agent Minefield research figure 2"></p><p id="block25"><i>Figure 2. Alice and Bob’s payoff matrices, where A and B are Alice’s and Bob’s action profiles.</i></p><p id="block26">allow for the easy computation of Nash equilibria and utility-maximizing outcomes (i.e., the outcome that maximizes the sum of payoffs).</p><p id="block27"><strong id="Step_three">Step three</strong></p><p id="block28">We had 15 frontier models play against copies of themselves, presenting the model with the “Alice” side of the story once and the “Bob” side once. After gathering the models’ two responses, we can say that a model has chosen one of the four outcomes. Our results give a conservative “lower bound”—if a model cannot even coordinate with itself, it will almost certainly fail to coordinate with a competitor<span data-footnote-reference="" data-footnote-index="1" data-footnote-id="-47rhgfzyaadWD8fjJ-1" role="doc-noteref" id="fnref-47rhgfzyaadWD8fjJ-1" class="footnote-reference"><sup><span><a href="#fn-47rhgfzyaadWD8fjJ-1" class="">[1]</a></span></sup></span>.</p><p id="block29"><strong id="Step_four">Step four</strong></p><p id="block30">We tested five interventions inspired by <span><span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanism_design#:~:text=Mechanism%20design%20(sometimes,information%20they%20have.">mechanism design</a></span></span> – the subfield of economics concerned with constructing rules to produce good outcomes according to some pre-defined metric – by pre-pending the ordinary system prompt with a narrative, nudging models towards more utilitarian outcomes. For example, we tried to make models believe they’d entered into <strong>contracts with penalties</strong> by adding system prompts that said, for example, “You’ve entered into a legal agreement to choose &lt;good outcome&gt;.”</p><hr/><h2 id="Results__Can_LLMs_coordinate_">Results: Can LLMs coordinate?</h2><p id="block31">For each model, we computed two key metrics:</p><ul><li id="block32"><i>Utilitarian accuracy</i>: fraction of samples where the LLM, through self-play, chooses a utility-maximising outcome. Baseline is 25%.</li><li id="block33"><i>Nash accuracy</i>: fraction of samples where the LLM through self-play chooses a Nash equilibrium. Baseline is 25%.</li></ul><p id="block34"><strong id="1__The_Hierarchy_of__Niceness_">1. The Hierarchy of &quot;Niceness&quot;</strong></p><p id="block35">Not all models are created equal. In our testing, <strong>Anthropic&#x27;s Claude 4.5</strong> models achieved the highest utilitarian accuracy, followed by <strong>Meta’s Llama 3.3</strong> and <strong>OpenAI’s GPT-5</strong>. Models from Google, Qwen, DeepSeek, and Grok tended to struggle more. Interestingly, being &quot;smarter&quot; (higher general capability) didn&#x27;t always make a model better at achieving utilitarian outcomes.</p><p id="block36"><img src="../assets/blog/the-multi-agent-minefield-can-llms-cooperate-to-avoid-global/figure-3.webp" srcSet="https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/jfgvg7wxpx4b7ekpxroz 160w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/sf6nlosucjepfr0fd5nd 320w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/u11saszbm4dj9v3f0pel 480w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/ckm6s8kjdrbbxi8tenrz 640w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/maji59vfmah7vfc69dfg 800w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/j8cbd2gajd3kescr3nmm 960w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/gjiy0cbdtqzyq7nugeqb 1120w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/epamn1mnayvwbht6ylf9 1280w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/nwv1qerqfhph1nmemdsn 1440w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/p72rpgfz7qom8k9lfzoe 1600w"/ width="1600" height="781" alt="Multi-Agent Minefield research figure 3"></p><p id="block37"><i>Figure 3. Utilitarian accuracy (fraction of actions maximizing total welfare, i.e. sum of utilities) across models and game types. Cell colors range from red (0.0) to green (1.0).</i></p><p id="block38"><strong id="2__The_Prisoner_s_Dilemma">2. The Prisoner’s Dilemma</strong></p><p id="block39">In <strong>Prisoner’s Dilemma</strong> scenarios (like an autonomous weapons arms race), models cooperated <strong>44% of the time</strong>, the random baseline being 25% and human studies revealing cooperation rates between <span><span><a href="https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-1-4614-7883-6_557-2?">40-60%</a></span></span>. Most defaulted to &quot;individually rational&quot; defection, even when it led to a worse outcome for everyone. They were much better at <strong>Chicken</strong>, where the utility-maximizing outcome was reached 80% of times.</p><p id="block40"><strong id="3__The__Game_Theory__Anchoring_Effect">3. The &quot;Game Theory&quot; Anchoring Effect</strong></p><p id="block41">Framing matters. When we moved from a prosaic narrative (a story about a climate summit) to a more game-theoretic framing (explicit numbers/payoffs), models became “colder&quot;. Anchoring is real, even for LLMs.</p><ul><li id="block42">They got <strong>better</strong> at finding the Nash Equilibrium (+6.2%).</li><li id="block43">They got <strong>worse</strong> at reaching the utilitarian outcome (-4.1%). Basically, the more &quot;math-like&quot; the prompt, the more the AI acts like a textbook egoist</li></ul><p id="block44"><strong id="4__Social_Engineering_Can_Steer_Towards_Good_Outcomes">4. Social Engineering Can Steer Towards Good Outcomes</strong></p><p id="block45">The good news is that we can sometimes steer these agents, pre-pending system prompts corresponding to the following interventions:</p><ul><li id="block46"><strong>Contracts with Penalties:</strong> Adding a binding agreement that punishes unilateral betrayal.</li><li id="block47"><strong>Trusted Mediators:</strong> Using a third party to suggest actions.</li><li id="block48"><strong>Side Payments:</strong> Allowing for &quot;bribes&quot; or transfers to reward cooperative behavior.</li></ul><p id="block49">These interventions improved utilitarian outcomes by <strong>14-18 percentage points</strong>, suggesting that safe multi-agent AI may depend less on &quot;training for niceness&quot; and more on building robust digital institutions.</p><p id="block50"><i><img src="../assets/blog/the-multi-agent-minefield-can-llms-cooperate-to-avoid-global/figure-4.webp" srcSet="https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/suokkuvvga950elkxzgd 160w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/ukfjmliqf2hm2kxagvg7 320w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/cl9s2fq59sxpjpldxrv5 480w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/kwca97m048tjioiqu8xk 640w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/eyxzxiatnwrebgmfte9p 800w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/kf0uwf2nlnzdkjuq4xoj 960w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/wtves7biy7pzegooeewc 1120w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/tyqmbj5kvyvgqbsmr14v 1280w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/xuzw0y2vtik0oadddape 1440w, https://res.cloudinary.com/lesswrong-2-0/image/upload/f_auto,q_auto/v1/mirroredImages/PtnD33ohrAju2DaPK/lcjpokueg7kvv6t9nog9 1600w"/ width="1600" height="844" alt="Multi-Agent Minefield research figure 4">Figure 6: Results for five mechanisms implemented across 2,009 games, with Mediator achieving the greatest improvement.</i></p><hr/><h2 id="Conclusion_and_Future_Work">Conclusion and Future Work</h2><p id="block51">Our benchmark shows that LLMs don’t always reach the most “utilitarian” outcomes – only in about 4 of 10 cases. However, efforts to steer outcomes proved fruitful, with increases of up to 18% across multiple interventions.</p><p id="block52"><strong id="Key_Uncertainties_">Key Uncertainties:</strong></p><ul><li id="block53"><strong>Self-Play Bias:</strong> Our results use self-play, which might actually <strong>overestimate</strong> cooperation in some games<span data-footnote-reference="" data-footnote-index="2" data-footnote-id="-47rhgfzyaadWD8fjJ-2" role="doc-noteref" id="fnref-47rhgfzyaadWD8fjJ-2" class="footnote-reference"><sup><span><a href="#fn-47rhgfzyaadWD8fjJ-2" class="">[2]</a></span></sup></span>.</li><li id="block54"><strong>2×2 games are simplifications:</strong> While 2×2 games capture core strategic tensions, they are not a faithful representation of the real world. Real conflicts involve hidden information, long time horizons, and thousands of players.</li></ul><p id="block55">This benchmark highlights significant reliability gaps in LLMs’ coordination abilities, but it also raises new questions to investigate:</p><ul><li id="block56"><strong>Inter-Model Diplomacy:</strong> How do coordination rates change when a Claude agent interacts with a Llama agent? Do &quot;reasoning mismatches&quot; lead to more frequent crashes in Chicken games?</li><li id="block57"><strong>Emergent Cooperation vs. Emergent Defection:</strong> Does increasing model scale eventually lead to &quot;emergence&quot; of better social coordination, or does it simply make models more efficient at pursuing their self-interest?</li><li id="block58"><strong>Other games:</strong> How about extending the analysis to other kinds of games?</li><li id="block59"><strong>Memory and Reputation:</strong> Real-world risks are rarely one-shot games. Future research could explore if models can develop reputational heuristics over repeated rounds to sustain cooperation.</li></ul><p id="block60"><i>We thank Louis Thomson and Sara Fish for useful feedback on this blog post.
